## Feedback

This is a strong proposal. The research question is sharp and maps directly onto a regression (labor force participation on PFL exposure), the motivation draws on three well-chosen sources tied specifically to the mechanism (job attachment right after birth), and the data section shows real evidence of data work — a properly formatted summary statistics table with sensible means, SDs, and an N that's internally consistent with the sample description. The empirical specification is written out in full mathematical form with every term defined, and the fixed-effects choices are explained with concrete examples ("California always having higher female labor force participation than Alabama") rather than asserted mechanically. The threats section is a highlight: it names the specific confounders, states the direction of the resulting bias, and separately treats measurement error and policy-induced migration — this is exactly the level of specificity the rubric rewards.

Two things need fixing before the final paper. First, the causal language is overstated twice: "This paper will show the causal effect of paid leave on mothers' employment" in the introduction, and "If β1 is positive and statistically significant, this will prove that paid leave increases mothers employment" in the planned analysis. Statistical significance in a TWFE regression does not "prove" a causal claim — it's consistent with one under the parallel-trends assumption, which the threats section itself flags as potentially violated. The fix is straightforward: replace "prove"/"will show the causal effect" with language tied to the identifying assumption, e.g., "if pre-trends are similar across adopting and non-adopting states, β1 can be interpreted as the effect of PFL on..." Second, there's a factual slip in the lit review — it refers to "these five peer-reviewed studies," but only four studies are actually discussed, and one of them (Bailey, Byker, Patel, & Ramnath 2019) is an NBER working paper, not a peer-reviewed article. That leaves the lit review with three peer-reviewed sources distinct from the introduction rather than the four required. The synthesis itself is genuinely good — it traces how the short-run findings (Rossin-Slater et al., Baum & Ruhm, Byker) contrast with the surprising long-run findings (Bailey et al.) and positions the paper's contribution (all states through 2023, not just California) — so this is a sourcing gap, not a synthesis problem.

Smaller issues: the reference list is missing an entry for Waldfogel (1998), which is cited in the introduction but not in the bibliography (only the 2013 Rossin-Slater/Ruhm/Waldfogel paper appears). The control variable list (age, bachelor's degree, marital status, number of children, Hispanic ethnicity) is presented without explaining why each belongs in the specification — and marital status and number of children are plausibly themselves affected by PFL availability, which would make them bad controls rather than confounders to net out. Two small grammar slips ("does not effect" for "affect"; "mothers employment" missing an apostrophe) are the only writing issues in an otherwise clean, professional draft.

## Next Steps for the Final Paper

**Identification and causal language.** The most important fix is tightening the causal framing. You have a reasonable identification strategy — a two-way fixed effects design exploiting staggered state adoption of PFL from 2004 (California) through 2023 (Oregon) — but two-way FE with staggered treatment timing is exactly the setting where the modern DiD literature (Goodman-Bacon, Callaway & Sant'Anna, Sun & Abraham) has shown that naive TWFE estimates can be contaminated by already-treated units acting as comparison groups. You don't need to implement one of the newer estimators for the final paper, but you should at least acknowledge the concern and, ideally, show an event-study plot of LFP around each state's adoption date to give readers a visual check on pre-trends. Note also that California (2004) and New Jersey (2009) are already treated for your entire 2010–2023 sample window — they contribute no within-sample timing variation, so your identification is really coming from the six later-adopting states (RI, NY, WA, MA, CT, OR) plus the never-treated states. That's a fine amount of variation to work with, but it's worth stating explicitly rather than leaving it implicit.

**Data work.** Add a group-comparison panel to your summary statistics — mean LFP and controls separately for state-years with PFL in effect vs. not — since this comparison is the heart of your design and readers will want to see the raw gap before you show them the regression-adjusted one. Also reconsider marital status and number of children as controls: if PFL availability could plausibly shift a mother's decision to marry or have another child, controlling for these introduces post-treatment bias rather than removing confounding. You could either drop them, control for a pre-birth/baseline value instead, or explicitly justify why you believe they're exogenous to PFL status in your sample.

**Literature.** You're one clean peer-reviewed source short of the requirement in the lit review as currently framed (since Bailey et al. is a working paper). An easy fix is to find a peer-reviewed piece on the newer state programs (New York, Washington, or Massachusetts) rather than relying so heavily on the California literature — this would also strengthen your "my paper looks beyond California" contribution claim with citations that back it up directly.

**Table plan.** For your results table, build it as a sequence that shows robustness of β1: Column 1 with just the PFL indicator and state/year FE, Column 2 adding the individual controls, Column 3 restricted to mothers whose youngest child is under one, Column 4 splitting by bachelor's degree (as you describe in Planned Analysis). This lets the reader see whether the coefficient is stable as controls are added — which is itself informative about how much omitted-variable bias is at play — and lines up your planned subgroup analyses with an actual table structure rather than leaving them as a narrative plan only.
