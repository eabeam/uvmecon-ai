## Feedback

Andy — this is a strong proposal, and I want to be clear about that before I get into the criticism. The research question is well-posed, the motivation is genuinely motivating (you tie stalled U.S. female LFP to *this specific policy lever*, rather than gesturing at "gender equality is important"), the data section shows you actually pulled and cleaned the extract, and your summary statistics table is one of the better ones I've seen — I spot-checked every binary variable's standard deviation against sqrt(p(1-p)) and they all match, which tells me these numbers came out of Stata and not out of thin air. Your threats section is the best part of the paper: you named specific omitted variables *and* signed the direction of the bias, which is the step almost everyone skips.

Here is the central problem, and it's a fixable one. **You have written a staggered-adoption difference-in-differences and you never call it that.** Once you name it, three questions become unavoidable, and right now none of them is addressed. First, parallel trends: you mention in one clause of Section 6 that "if PFL states were already on a different trend before adopting the policy, the bias would remain" — that is exactly right, and it deserves a test, not a clause. Second, **California and New Jersey adopted in 2004 and 2009, both before your sample starts in 2010.** They are always-treated in your window, so they contribute nothing to identifying β₁ — your entire estimate is coming off Rhode Island, New York, Washington, Massachusetts, Connecticut, and Oregon. That's not a fatal problem (arguably it's your contribution — you say yourself you want results that aren't just about California), but you need to know it and say it, because right now a reader assumes CA is in the identification when it isn't. Third, with six adoption dates and effects that plausibly differ across cohorts, plain two-way fixed effects doesn't estimate what you think it estimates — the Goodman-Bacon decomposition shows it puts negative weight on some already-treated comparisons.

Two smaller things that will cost you real points if they carry into the final paper. **Your causal language is inconsistent with your own analysis.** Section 6 is honest and careful; Sections 1 and 5 say the paper "will show the causal effect" and that a significant coefficient "will prove that paid leave increases mothers employment." A significant coefficient never proves anything, and if you'd let your Section 6 self edit your Section 1, this wouldn't have happened. Cut "prove" out of your vocabulary. And **your literature review is short by one peer-reviewed source**: you write "These five peer-reviewed studies," but you discuss four, and Bailey et al. (2019) is an NBER working paper — your own bibliography says so. You need one more published article. You're also missing Waldfogel (1998) from your reference list even though you cite it in the intro.

## Next Steps for the Final Paper

**Research question — keep it, but lead with the sharp version.** Your opening sentence ("How does paid family leave affect mothers?") is broader than what you actually do. Open with the question you're actually answering: "Do state paid family leave programs increase labor force participation among mothers of children under five?" Then state the hypothesis and the expected sign explicitly, so the reader knows what a rejection would look like.

**Identification — this is where the paper is won.** The realistic path is: keep the TWFE model as your baseline, then add three things. (1) **An event study.** Define event time relative to each state's adoption year, estimate coefficients for roughly k = −4 to +5 with k = −1 omitted, and plot them. If the pre-period coefficients are flat and near zero, you have earned the right to interpret β₁ causally, and that figure will become the most important exhibit in the paper. If they trend, you've learned something important and you report it honestly. (2) **A robustness estimator** — Callaway–Sant'Anna (`csdid` in Stata) or Sun–Abraham (`eventstudyinteract`) — to show your result isn't an artifact of TWFE weighting under staggered adoption. You don't need to master the econometrics of these; you need to show the estimate survives. (3) **Cluster your standard errors at the state level.** With treatment assigned at the state-year level, unclustered SEs will make you look far more precise than you are. With ~51 clusters you're in reasonable territory; report it explicitly. Also note somewhere that you're running a linear probability model, so use robust/clustered SEs and acknowledge that fitted values aren't bounded in [0,1] — a logit robustness check is a cheap footnote.

**Data work — four concrete additions.** (a) **Split the summary stats table by PFL status** (ever-PFL vs. never-PFL states, or treated vs. untreated state-years), with a difference column. That is the balance table your design implies, and its absence is conspicuous. (b) **Fix `nchild`** — call it "Number of own children." (c) **Check your Oregon coding.** My understanding is that Paid Leave Oregon began paying benefits in September 2023 (please verify against the statute), which would mean Oregon was *not* treated during the March 2023 CPS reference week. A one-state, one-year miscode won't move β₁ much, but a reader who knows the policy timeline will catch it. Consider dropping partial-treatment years or coding fractional exposure. (d) **Report how many observations you dropped** for missing education/marital status, and whether the droppers look different — one sentence is enough.

**Controls — one flag, as a teaching moment.** `nchild` (number of own children) is a potential **bad control**. PFL is a birth-related policy, and there is an active literature on whether paid leave affects fertility. If PFL changes how many children a woman has, then conditioning on completed fertility partially blocks the very channel you're trying to measure — classic post-treatment bias. Marital status has a milder version of the same problem. My suggestion: show your main table both with and without these controls. If β₁ is stable, you've defused the issue in one row; if it moves, you have something to write about.

**Literature — add one published paper and rewrite for synthesis.** You need a fourth peer-reviewed source in the lit review (Bailey et al. is NBER). Good candidates: Ruhm's cross-national work on leave mandates and female employment, Rossin-Slater's *JHR*/handbook treatment of leave policy, or Stearns on Temporary Disability Insurance. More importantly, **restructure the section around the tension you've already found.** Right now it's four paragraph-length annotations in a row. The interesting fact is that Rossin-Slater et al. and Baum & Ruhm find positive short-run effects while Bailey et al. find *negative* effects ten years out. Organize the review around that: what's known in the short run, what's contested in the long run, and where your all-states, 2010–2023 design sits. That single reframe moves you from summary to synthesis.

**Table plan for the final paper.** Table 1: summary stats, split by PFL status. Table 2 (main results): Column 1 = PFL indicator with state and year FE only; Column 2 = add individual controls; Column 3 = drop the potentially-bad controls (`nchild`, married) as a robustness column; Column 4 = Callaway–Sant'Anna estimate. Table 3 (heterogeneity): the same specification split by youngest child under 1 vs. 1–4, and by bachelor's degree vs. not — you've already motivated both cuts from Byker (2016), so carry that motivation into the table notes. Figure 1: the event-study plot. That's a complete, publishable-shaped paper.

**Writing.** Do one pass for the mechanical errors — "effect" for "affect," the missing apostrophe in "mothers employment," the comma splice at "…happened to them, however I expect…," and the β₁/β1 inconsistency. Then do a second pass hunting only for causal overclaims, and delete every one you find.
