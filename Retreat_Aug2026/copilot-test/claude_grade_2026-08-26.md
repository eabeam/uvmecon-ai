## Feedback

This is a strong proposal, Andy — one of the more complete designs I've seen at this stage. You have a real question, real data that you've clearly loaded and explored (the sample restrictions, the N, the PFL coding by effective date, and the summary statistics all show that), and a specification that matches the question. Your discussion of what the state and year fixed effects do is exactly the kind of reasoning I want to see: "The year fixed effects absorb national shocks such as the 2020 pandemic, which is important because several states adopted PFL around that time" tells me you understand *why* the fixed effects belong there, not just that they're conventional. The threats section is also genuinely specific — you name the omitted variables (state wealth, politics, female labor demand), you say which way the bias runs, and you correctly identify that fixed effects don't rescue you if PFL states were already on a different trend. Nice work.

The biggest thing to fix is a mismatch between your threats section and your claims elsewhere. In the introduction you write "This paper will show the causal effect of paid leave on mothers' employment," and in the planned analysis, "If β1 is positive and statistically significant, this will prove that paid leave increases mothers employment." Then, two sections later, you correctly explain that a differential pre-trend would leave the bias in place. Both can't be true. Your design is a generalized difference-in-differences with staggered adoption, and its credibility rests on the parallel-trends assumption — which you can *check* (event-study plot, pre-adoption leads) but never prove. Say that. "If the pre-trends look flat and β₁ is positive, this is evidence that PFL raises mothers' labor force participation" is both more honest and more persuasive. Related: you should say what your unit of policy variation is and cluster standard errors at the state level. With 51 clusters and a state-year treatment, unclustered SEs will badly overstate precision.

Two more things. First, the literature review: you have three peer-reviewed sources in that section, not five (Bailey et al. 2019 is an NBER working paper, which does not count as peer-reviewed for this assignment; and there are four papers in the section, not five). You need at least one more peer-reviewed source, and — more importantly — the section reads as four separate summaries with one positioning sentence at the end. Rework it around themes: short-run attachment effects (Rossin-Slater et al.; Baum & Ruhm; Byker) versus long-run effects (Bailey et al.), and heterogeneity by education, then say what's unknown (newer states, whether the California results generalize). Second, the summary statistics: rename `nchild` to something readable, and add columns for state-years with and without PFL. In a DiD-style design, the reader's first question is "how different are the treated and untreated groups at baseline?" — Table 1 should answer it. One data check while you're in there: 31 percent of observations in a PFL state-year seems high to me given the population shares of the PFL states over 2010–2023 (California and New Jersey for all 14 years, the others for only a few). Re-verify your PFL coding against the March reference week — a program that starts paying benefits mid-year should not be coded as "in effect" for that year's ASEC — and check whether D.C. (which is in your 51) should be coded as a PFL jurisdiction.

## Next Steps for the Final Paper

**Research question.** The question is sharp enough. Drop or rewrite the opening line ("How does paid family leave affect mothers?") so the first sentence a reader sees is the specific one: does the availability of state paid family leave raise labor force participation among mothers of children under five? State your hypothesis explicitly (β₁ > 0, on the order of 2–4 percentage points based on the California estimates) rather than only in the planned-analysis section.

**Identification strategy.** Name your design for what it is — a two-way fixed effects difference-in-differences with staggered adoption — and be clear about where the identifying variation comes from. California (2004) and New Jersey (2009) are treated in every year of your 2010–2023 window, so the state fixed effects absorb them entirely; β₁ is identified off the states that switch during your window (Rhode Island, New York, Washington, Massachusetts, Connecticut, Oregon, and possibly D.C.). That's fine, but you should say so, and a robustness column that drops CA and NJ would be reassuring. The most valuable addition is an event-study version of your equation: replace PFL_st with indicators for years relative to adoption (say, −4 through +4) and plot the coefficients. Flat pre-adoption coefficients are your best evidence for parallel trends; that figure will do more work than any paragraph. Two further items: cluster standard errors by state, and add a sentence acknowledging that with staggered adoption and possibly heterogeneous effects across cohorts, the TWFE coefficient is a weighted average that can be hard to interpret — a stacked or never-treated-control comparison is a reasonable robustness check if you have time, but at minimum flag it as a limitation. Also, think about your controls as a teaching moment on post-treatment bias: marital status, number of children, and "youngest child under one" could all respond to PFL (paid leave can shift the timing or number of births), so present results with and without them, and note that even the sample definition (mothers of children under five) is conditioned on a fertility decision the policy could affect.

**Data work.** (1) Add treated/untreated columns (and ideally a difference column) to Table 1. (2) Replace `nchild` with "Number of own children." (3) Decide whether to use ASEC person weights; if you stay unweighted, say why and show a weighted robustness column. (4) Re-audit the PFL indicator: confirm each program was actually paying benefits by the March reference week of the year you code it as on, and reconcile the 31 percent share with the population shares of the PFL states. (5) Add a state-level panel table or figure showing which states adopt and when — it helps the reader see the identifying variation.

**Literature.** Add at least one peer-reviewed source to the lit review — a study of a non-California program (New Jersey or New York), a paper on fertility or birth-timing responses to PFL (which also motivates the post-treatment-control discussion), or a review article would all fit. Check whether Bailey et al. (2019) has since appeared in a journal; if it has, cite the published version. Add Waldfogel (1998) to the references. Restructure the section thematically rather than paper-by-paper, and end with a clear statement of what your paper adds: national coverage, later adopters, and a test of whether the California findings generalize.

**Table plan.** Table 2 (main results): Column 1, PFL indicator with state and year FE only; Column 2, add individual controls; Column 3, add state-specific linear trends; Column 4, drop CA and NJ. Table 3 (heterogeneity): youngest child under one vs. one-to-four; bachelor's degree vs. not — each with the Column 2 specification. Figure 1: event-study coefficients with 95% confidence intervals, SEs clustered by state. Fix the small writing slips (affect/effect; mothers'; the "five" count; the comma splice before "however"), and replace "prove" and "will show the causal effect" with hedged language throughout.
